# Standard Model

The Standard Model category encompasses the quantum structure of matter and the universe at all scales: from elementary particles to the cosmological evolution of the universe. In strict particle-physics usage, the Standard Model refers specifically to the SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1) gauge theory of elementary particles; here the name is used broadly to encompass the quantum description of matter at all scales. It is relevant whenever the behavior of matter cannot be described by classical fields alone: atomic spectra, nuclear decay, particle collisions, neutrino oscillations, or the thermal history of the early universe.

## Brief history

1. 1900: Planck's blackbody law introduced quantized energy exchange.
2. 1913: Bohr quantized atomic orbits, explaining spectral lines.
3. 1925–1927: Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Dirac, and Born formulated modern quantum mechanics.
4. 1960s–1970s: the Standard Model of particle physics unified the electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions; the Big Bang model was established observationally.

## Categorization

The subtopics follow the scale of the physical system under consideration: from the most fundamental constituents (elementary particles) through their strong-interaction bound states (nuclei) to electromagnetic bound states (atoms), and finally to the universe as a whole (cosmology, the limiting case where no single bound system dominates). The prerequisite formalism is [Quantum mechanics](../Quantum-Mechanics/Readme.md), which provides the language (states, operators, amplitudes) in which all four subtopics are expressed.

<details><summary>Details</summary>

The four subtopics are ordered by the scale of the physical system they describe. At the smallest scale, [elementary particles](./Elementary-Particles.md) are the irreducible excitations of quantum fields, constrained by the gauge group SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1) of the [Interactions](../Interactions/Readme.md). At the nuclear scale, the strong interaction binds quarks into hadrons and nucleons into nuclei; the weak interaction governs beta decay and neutrino processes — together these form [nuclear physics](./Nuclear-Physics.md). At the atomic scale, the electromagnetic interaction binds electrons to nuclei, producing the periodic table, spectral lines, and chemical bonding — [atomic physics](./Atomic-Physics.md). At the largest scale, the same physics governs the thermal evolution of the universe from the first fractions of a second to the present: [cosmology](./Cosmology.md).

Note: the Standard Model does not include gravity. General relativity, described in the [Interactions](../Interactions/Readme.md) category, governs spacetime curvature and is not yet unified with the quantum framework of the Standard Model.

The prerequisite formalism is [Quantum mechanics](../Quantum-Mechanics/Readme.md): states are vectors in Hilbert space, observables are operators, and measurement yields probabilistic outcomes. In the large-action limit, quantum mechanics reproduces [Mechanics](../Mechanics/Readme.md) (correspondence principle). The Standard Model extends this to relativistic quantum fields, where particles are created and destroyed.
